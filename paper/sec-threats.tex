\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}
Following Jedlitschka et al.~\cite{jedlitschka2008reporting} and Pinto et al.~\cite{pinto2014energy}, each threat is paired with a mitigation, an ancillary experiment that bounds it, or a statement of its effect on the results. The ancillary experiments are \textbf{AX1}, refresh cadence versus \texttt{powermetrics} interval (done, Section~\ref{sec:meas-energy}); \textbf{AX2}, refresher overhead (preliminary); \textbf{AX3}, IOReport versus \texttt{powermetrics} CPU power (read path); \textbf{AX4}, load scaling with 0 to 10 busy threads (linearity); \textbf{AX5}, calibration of $d$ ($10^4$ sleeps per thread kind and platform; SOCKET round trip); \textbf{AX6}, E-core confinement under BACKGROUND QoS and its frequency effect; and \textbf{AX7}, drift of per-run energy with run index.

\subsection{Construct Validity}
\textbf{Estimated energy.} No wall meter is available. RAPL is validated against physical measurement~\cite{khan2018rapl}; Apple's counters are model estimates without published validation, and \texttt{powermetrics} values are software estimates that Apple advises against comparing across devices~\cite{hubner2025apple}. Hypotheses compare treatments within one machine, so a common bias cancels; AX3 and AX4 check the read path and linearity; absolute accuracy remains unverified.
\textbf{Scope and static power.} The counters miss the power supply, SSD, fans, and network, and waiting threads leave the Mac CPU clusters nearly idle (about 0.02\,W), so the cost of waiting longer is mainly static power, seen only in part; hence the idle-subtracted and (Mac) memory-subsystem tests, the SoC report, and a stated limitation.
\textbf{Analytic time floor.} On blocking workloads VT's time advantage over pools with $p < 256$ holds by construction (Section~\ref{sec:planning-variables}), so tests use equal concurrency and smaller pools are reported relative to their floor.
\textbf{Counter staleness} (about 2\% of a window, Section~\ref{sec:meas-energy}) is under half the $\pm 5\%$ equivalence margin, and the profiler fails if the counters stop advancing.
\textbf{Sleep as an I/O proxy.} \texttt{Thread.sleep} involves no device, and its macOS latency depends on system activity (Section~\ref{sec:meas-pitfalls}); AX5 calibrates $d$, and SOCKET adds real loopback I/O.
\textbf{Energy per operation.} Pool creation and teardown are included by definition, which favors SEQ and VT (whose carrier pool persists); JFR thread-start counts bound this, and the SOCKET server's share is measured separately (Section~\ref{sec:planning-units}). \upd{The threat materialized: on blocking workloads the cost of creating the pools dominated FP and FJ, so the reused-pool control reports both pool lifetimes (Section~\ref{sec:res-rq2}); the SOCKET server's idle cost was below the detection limit (SRV: 0.0107, 0.0092, and 0.0111\,W idle without, with, and again without the server).}
\textbf{Implementations.} PT and recursive FJ differ from the 2014 code, so 2014 comparisons concern shapes and rankings.
\textbf{QoS is not pure placement.} BACKGROUND QoS also lowers frequency and I/O priority and may widen timer coalescing; AX6 and residency logs quantify the former, AX5 calibrates $d$ under both QoS classes, and RQ3.1 on the Mac measures QoS confinement, not core type alone; the asymmetry claim rests on the Intel equal-count arm. \upd{AX6 confirmed full confinement (P-cluster residency 0\%) and an E-cluster clock of 1090\,MHz instead of 2752\,MHz; AX5 measured 10\,ms sleeps lasting 177\,ms (median) under BACKGROUND.}
\upd{\textbf{Package scope on Intel.} RAPL package energy includes uncore and about 3.3\,W of idle power, whereas the Mac counter covers only the CPU clusters; Mac and Intel energy values are therefore not comparable, and only rankings and ratios are compared.}

\subsection{Internal Validity}
\textbf{Background load} (Section~\ref{sec:meas-pitfalls}) is controlled by the quiet-machine protocol, the idle gate, and the two gated idle baselines per batch.
\textbf{Run order and power state.} The A1 Intel pilot confounded run order with power state (Section~\ref{sec:meas-pitfalls}). As innocuous setup changes bias measurements~\cite{mytkowicz2009wrong}, we hold the setup fixed and randomize all runs at fork level; Intel CPU-bound runs heat-soak into the sustained regime with per-iteration frequency logging, Mac runs cool down to ``Nominal'' thermal pressure, and AX7 tests for drift. \upd{AX7 found within-cell drift with run order on SLEEP (Spearman $\rho = 0.30$) and SOCKET ($\rho = 0.20$), not on CPU ($\rho = 0.06$, $p = 0.28$); batch idle power rose from about 0.009 to 0.012\,W over the 15\,h Mac campaign. The randomized order turns this drift into noise rather than bias.}
\upd{\textbf{Disruptions.} The Mac stayed on Wi-Fi; the first Mac campaign was restarted after an OS update (pitfall~(7)); the Intel campaign was resumed twice, and its idle power rose from 3.2 to 4.0\,W between sessions, while every batch's idle pair agreed.}
\textbf{Measurement overhead.} Boundary-only reads avoid polling-induced idle-state suppression~\cite{raffin2025dissecting}, the refresher's overhead (AX2) is constant and part of the idle baseline, and profilers never run during energy runs.
\textbf{JIT and GC.} JMH warmup, a fresh JVM per fork, and a fixed heap stabilize the runtime; construct-induced GC is part of the cost, and JFR attributes it.
\textbf{Uncontrolled DVFS.} Frequencies cannot be fixed on the Mac, and Intel keeps HWP and turbo on, as deployed; frequency is part of the treatment effect and is logged.
\textbf{Core count on Intel.} \texttt{taskset} also changes the processor count the JVM sees, and with it the GC and JIT thread counts; equal-count arms keep these equal across core types.
\textbf{Unexplained FJ behavior.} Mac pilot FJ/SLEEP was faster than FP/SLEEP with equal workers, its CPU energy/op varied strongly (CV 116\%), and one fork ran at FP speed. \upd{FJ is now bounded at $p$ threads; JDK~25 did not add compensation threads, so the cap is a safeguard and does not explain the anomaly. In the main campaign FJ/SLEEP at $p = 16$ had a CV of 6\%; the JFR profiles are in the replication package but were not analyzed in time.}

\subsection{External Validity}
\textbf{Two machines.} Results come from one Mac mini M4, a mainstream P/E design, and one Intel Core Ultra~7 255H, a three-tier x86 design; cross-platform comparisons concern rankings and curve shapes only (Section~\ref{sec:meas-procedure}). \textbf{Workloads.} Three synthetic kernels with 256 fixed, independent tasks replace the nine 2014 benchmarks; they span CPU-bound, blocking, and network I/O behavior but characterize mechanisms, not application savings. \textbf{Runtime.} One JDK (25, long-term support), one collector, and one OS per platform; results apply to this configuration. \upd{\textbf{Reduced Intel grid.} Intel covers $p \in \{4, 16, 256\}$ and $p = 4$ on the pinned arms only.}

\subsection{Conclusion Validity}
We use non-parametric tests chosen a priori for \textbf{small samples of unknown distribution}, Holm correction within families for \textbf{multiple comparisons}, effect sizes with bootstrap CIs to separate \textbf{significance from magnitude}, and TOST ($\pm 5\%$) before claiming \textbf{null results}. The fork is the unit, which avoids \textbf{pseudo-replication}~\cite{georges2007rigorous}; a split sample by fixed fork index removes \textbf{selection bias in H0-1.2}; a power check on pilot variance confirms the \textbf{sample size}; and \textbf{noise-floor} blocking-workload energy is tested idle-subtracted with a detection limit (Section~\ref{sec:planning-analysis}). \upd{Two limits remain: the idle-subtracted blocking contrasts among FP, FJ, and VT have low power (0.04 to 0.13), and the Intel arm has 3 forks per cell, so its results are descriptive and claims rest on non-overlapping fork ranges.}
