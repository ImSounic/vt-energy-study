\begin{abstract}
The last systematic measurement of the energy consumed by Java's thread management constructs dates from 2014, before virtual threads (JDK~21) and before hybrid processors whose core asymmetry the JVM scheduler cannot see. We compare five constructs (sequential execution, one platform thread per task, fixed thread pools, Fork/Join, and virtual threads) at concurrency levels from 1 to 256 under CPU-bound, blocking-sleep, and loopback-socket workloads on two platforms: an Apple~M4 measured with IOReport counters (930 runs, plus a 280-run control) and an Intel Core Ultra~7 255H measured with RAPL (a reduced campaign of 318 runs with equal-count core pinning). On CPU-bound work, parallel execution used 2.4 (M4) to 4.0 (Intel) times less energy than sequential execution, the opposite of the 2014 finding, and at equal concurrency and clock frequency the construct changed energy/op by at most 3\%. Faster was greener on the M4 (Spearman $\rho = 0.99$) but not on Intel ($\rho = 0.45$), where configurations that create threads continuously ran at the 25\,W package limit and were up to 33\% faster but used up to 38\% more energy. On blocking workloads, virtual threads used 15 to 36 times less CPU energy than the platform-thread constructs at 256 threads; a control with thread pools reused across operations showed that most of the gap between pools and virtual threads is the cost of creating threads, while virtual threads with few carriers remained the lowest-energy configuration on Intel. Pinning showed that the same thread-per-task workload costs 1.5 times more energy on two P cores than on two E cores.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
How a Java program manages its threads shapes how much energy it consumes, yet the standard reference on the energy behavior of Java's thread management constructs is more than a decade old. Pinto, Castor, and Liu measured explicit threads, thread pools, and Fork/Join on JDK~1.7 on homogeneous machines; for six of their nine benchmarks the \emph{sequential} version consumed the least energy, and they concluded that ``being `faster' clearly has little correlation with being `greener' for concurrent programs on multicore architectures''~\cite{pinto2014energy}.

Two developments since then limit extrapolation from that baseline. First, JDK~21 finalized \emph{virtual threads}~\cite{jep444}, which multiplex many lightweight threads over a small carrier \texttt{ForkJoinPool} and unmount a thread from its carrier whenever it blocks. Second, mainstream CPUs became \emph{hybrid}: Intel mixes performance and efficiency (P/E) cores, recently with a third low-power tier, and Apple's M-series processors also combine P and E cores. The carrier scheduler treats all carriers as equivalent, although the same work costs different energy on a P core than on an E core; the energy cost of this unawareness has not been measured.

\textbf{No published study measures the energy consumption of JEP~444 virtual threads against platform threads, fixed thread pools, and Fork/Join on JDK~21+, and none on a hybrid processor with heterogeneous core types.} Three observations support this gap. (a)~The 2014 baseline~\cite{pinto2014energy} predates both Project Loom and hybrid cores. (b)~The closest hybrid-core JVM energy work~\cite{shimchenko2024scheduling} varies only the placement of garbage-collection worker threads. (c)~The two energy studies of virtual threads we found fall short of a construct-level comparison: one switches virtual threads on or off in a single web application~\cite{stocker2026energyupgrades}, and the other compares two arms on a microcontroller with a stack (OpenJDK~21 with ZGC) that cannot run as described~\cite{shanjaikumar2026virtualthreads}.

This report refines our proposal (Assignment~1, A1) \upd{and experiment plan (A2) and reports the executed study} on two platforms: the primary Apple~M4 provides the per-cluster energy split and a mainstream P/E design without affinity control, and the three-tier Intel platform of the proposal adds RAPL counters validated against meters, core pinning with equal core counts, and the psys domain. The plan adds a sequential baseline, controls the concurrency level of every pooled construct (including the carrier count of virtual threads), adds a loopback socket workload, and confines execution to efficiency cores. \upd{We executed 930 runs on the M4, a 280-run control that reuses thread pools across operations, and a reduced Intel campaign of 318 runs. Parallel execution used 2.4 to 4.0 times less energy than sequential execution on CPU-bound work, reversing the 2014 baseline; faster was greener on the M4 but not on Intel, where thread churn raised clock frequencies; and the blocking-workload advantage of virtual threads came mostly from avoiding thread creation, which reused pools also avoid.} Section~\ref{sec:context} gives the context, Section~\ref{sec:related} the related work, Section~\ref{sec:planning} the experiment plan following Jedlitschka et al.~\cite{jedlitschka2008reporting}, Section~\ref{sec:measurement} the measurement tools, environment, and procedure following the primer of Castor~\cite{castor2024primer}, Section~\ref{sec:threats} the threats to validity, \upd{Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} the discussion, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concluding remarks}.

\section{Context}\label{sec:context}
\subsection{Technical Context}\label{sec:technical}
\emph{Constructs.} We compare \changed{five} thread management constructs on \changed{JDK~25, the current long-term-support release}: \changed{(i)~sequential execution in the caller thread;} (ii)~platform threads created per task; (iii)~fixed-size thread pools (\texttt{newFixedThreadPool}); (iv)~Fork/Join work stealing; and (v)~virtual threads (thread-per-task over a carrier pool)~\cite{jep444}. \changed{Constructs (ii) to (iv) are those} of the 2014 baseline\changed{, which used sequential execution as its reference}~\cite{pinto2014energy}; the \changed{fifth} did not exist then. The constructs differ in the properties that plausibly drive energy: how many OS threads exist (per-task creation can oversubscribe the machine, a fixed pool caps it), how work is distributed (central queue versus per-worker deques with stealing), and what happens on blocking (a parked platform thread remains an OS-scheduled thread; a blocked virtual thread unmounts, freeing its carrier). \changed{The concurrency level is the worker count of the fixed pool and of Fork/Join and the carrier count of virtual threads; sequential execution and per-task platform threads have none.} \upd{FP and FJ create and shut down their pool inside every operation, whereas the carrier pool of VT persists for the JVM's lifetime; because this difference turned out to dominate the blocking workloads, a control experiment also runs FP and FJ with one pool reused across operations (Section~\ref{sec:planning-design}).}

\emph{Platform.} \changed{The primary platform is an Apple Mac mini~M4 with 16\,GB of unified memory under macOS~27. Its 10 cores form two clusters: 4 performance (P) cores reaching about 4.4\,GHz and 6 efficiency (E) cores reaching about 2.9\,GHz. macOS offers no CPU-affinity API; the only placement lever visible to developers is the quality-of-service class, and the background class (\texttt{taskpolicy -b}) confines a process to the E cores. The second arm, piloted on 2~October, keeps the platform of our proposal,} an Intel Core Ultra~7 255H, a hybrid CPU with \emph{three} core tiers and no SMT: 6 P-cores (up to 5.1\,GHz, Linux capacity 1024), 8 E-cores (4.4\,GHz, 774), and 2 LP-E cores (2.5\,GHz, 312). \changed{Unlike macOS, Linux lets placement be fixed per core with \texttt{taskset}, so the Intel arm carries the core-asymmetry claim and the Mac arm the per-cluster energy split.}

\emph{Measurement.} \changed{On the Mac, energy is read from Apple's IOReport energy-model counters: cumulative estimates maintained by the system on chip (SoC) at 1\,mJ resolution for the CPU clusters (with per-core detail), the memory subsystem (DRAM and memory controller), the GPU, and other SoC blocks. On Intel,} energy is read from the RAPL counters exposed via the Linux powercap interface, at four domains: package, core, uncore, and psys. The measurement itself can perturb the system: high-frequency RAPL polling suppresses C-states and can inflate idle power by tens of watts at kilohertz rates~\cite{raffin2025dissecting}. Our profiler therefore reads counters \emph{only at benchmark-iteration boundaries}\changed{, through one code path on both platforms}, adding no periodic wake-ups during the measured region. \changed{On macOS~27, however, the CPU counters advance only while \texttt{powermetrics} refreshes them; Section~\ref{sec:measurement} shows how a 100\,ms refresh bounds the resulting error.}

\subsection{Societal Context and Sustainability}\label{sec:societal}
We frame impact with the five-dimension sustainability model of the Karlskrona Manifesto~\cite{becker2015karlskrona}. \emph{Environmental:} data centers consumed an estimated 415\,TWh (about 1.5\% of global electricity) in 2024, a figure projected to roughly double by 2030~\cite{iea2025energyai}; Java is one of the dominant server-side languages in that fleet, so per-operation energy differences multiply across long-running services. \emph{Economic:} energy is a first-order datacenter cost, and battery life a product constraint on laptops and mobile devices\changed{, which now ship hybrid CPUs}. \emph{Technical:} virtual threads improve maintainability by letting simple blocking code replace complex asynchronous code. \emph{Individual:} the same shift lowers developer cognitive load. \emph{Social:} the guidance serves Java's developer community\changed{, which must now choose among these constructs without energy data}.

These dimensions are in \emph{tension}, and we name one explicitly: virtual threads improve technical and individual sustainability (a simple blocking style), but may \emph{harm} environmental sustainability if near-free threads encourage over-parallelism that raises active-core counts and package power. Whether that trade-off is real is what the study quantifies\changed{, through the energy of virtual threads across concurrency levels and its relation to execution time (Section~\ref{sec:planning})}.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
We position \changed{this study} against \upd{twelve} studies; Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes the coverage gap.

\emph{Pinto et al.}~\cite{pinto2014energy} is our baseline: three constructs on JDK~1.7, measured with external NI data-acquisition current meters at 100 samples/s on two homogeneous AMD machines under the \texttt{ondemand} governor. \emph{Limitation:} JDK~7 on homogeneous hardware, no on-chip (RAPL) measurement, and results confounded with a governor long since replaced.

\upd{\emph{Pinto et al.}~\cite{pinto2016collections} measured the energy of Java's thread-safe collections under concurrent access and found that choosing among implementations with equivalent functionality can change energy consumption considerably. \emph{Limitation:} collections rather than thread management constructs, on homogeneous machines before virtual threads.}

\emph{Shimchenko et al.}~\cite{shimchenko2024scheduling} is the closest hybrid-core JVM energy study: pinning ZGC worker threads to E-cores on Alder Lake saved about 3\% energy, measured with RAPL. \emph{Limitation:} it varies only runtime (GC) threads, not application-level concurrency constructs.

\emph{Shanjai Kumar et al.}~\cite{shanjaikumar2026virtualthreads} \changed{attribute} energy differences to virtual threads \changed{on a battery-powered IoT device}. \emph{Limitation:} it compares only two arms, on a microcontroller, and reports a stack (OpenJDK~21 with ZGC on 192\,KB of SRAM) that cannot run as described.

\changed{\emph{Stocker and Wahler}~\cite{stocker2026energyupgrades} benchmark a Spring PetClinic REST service on Xeon, Ryzen, and Apple~M1 machines and find that newer JVMs and enabling virtual threads (Java~21 and~23) save substantial energy without code changes. \emph{Limitation:} a single I/O-bound CRUD workload, per-process software energy estimates (JoularJX), virtual threads switched on or off rather than compared with thread pools or Fork/Join, and no control of core placement on the hybrid M1.}

\changed{\emph{Siroti{\'c} et al.}~\cite{sirotic2025virtual} compare Java~21 virtual threads with an executor over platform threads, Fork/Join, and parallel streams on a CPU-bound prime-counting task, and find virtual threads slightly slower than the platform-thread executor. \emph{Limitation:} runtime only with no energy measurement, a single toy workload, and no hybrid cores.}

\emph{van Kempen et al.}~\cite{vankempen2025green} show that across 13 language implementations, power was statistically constant ($\approx$189.8\,W) once active cores and memory activity were controlled, so energy tracks time. \emph{Limitation:} a homogeneous Xeon with CLBG, SPEC CPU 2017, and DaCapo benchmarks, and the authors explicitly flag ``architectures mixing energy-saving and high-performance cores'' (our platform) as a case where their results may differ.

\changed{\emph{Faster versus greener.} Three further studies bear on RQ1.3 (Section~\ref{sec:planning}).} \emph{Pereira et al.}~\cite{pereira2017energy} is the standard language-energy ranking; \emph{Kim et al.}~\cite{kim2015racing} analyze race-to-idle heuristics, where racing can cost up to 3.34$\times$ the optimal energy and was worst on the platform with the \emph{lowest} idle power; and \emph{Arag{\'o}n-Jurado et al.}~\cite{aragonjurado2025faster} found a compiler-flag combination that made an iOS application 9\% faster and 34\% \emph{more} energy-hungry. \changed{\emph{Limitations:}} Pereira et al.\ carry instrumentation defects (counter-overflow mishandling, negative energy readings in the released data) documented by van Kempen et al.~\cite{vankempen2025green}. Kim et al.\ use a DVFS-under-deadline framing, not language-level constructs. Arag{\'o}n-Jurado et al.\ vary compiler flags on iOS, not concurrency constructs.

\emph{Raffin and Trystram}~\cite{raffin2025dissecting} dissect software energy measurement and show that high-frequency RAPL polling suppresses C-states, inflating idle power by tens of watts at 1\,kHz. \emph{Limitation:} pure measurement methodology; it answers no concurrency question.

\changed{\emph{H{\"u}bner et al.}~\cite{hubner2025apple} characterize the performance and power of the Apple M1 to M4 SoCs on HPC kernels, reading power with \texttt{powermetrics}; it is the closest precedent for energy measurement on our primary platform. \emph{Limitation:} HPC kernels rather than Java or concurrency constructs, and \texttt{powermetrics} is never validated against an external meter; the authors state that its values are software estimates.}

\emph{Novelty.} No prior study combines construct-level comparison, virtual threads, JDK~21+, \changed{energy measurement,} and heterogeneous cores (Table~\ref{tab:related}). \changed{The planned study addresses precisely that combination.}
