\begin{abstract}
The last systematic measurement of the energy consumed by Java's thread management constructs dates from 2014, before two major shifts: virtual threads (JEP~444, finalized in JDK~21) and hybrid processors that mix performance, efficiency, and low-power cores whose asymmetry the JVM scheduler cannot see. No published study measures the energy of virtual threads against platform threads, fixed thread pools, and Fork--Join on JDK~21+, and none on a processor with heterogeneous core types. We propose a comparative experiment, structured by the Goal--Question--Metric approach, that measures four concurrency constructs across two workload classes (CPU-bound and I/O-bound) and controlled core placements (P, E, LP-E) on a three-tier hybrid CPU (Intel Core Ultra~7 255H), using RAPL energy counters read only at benchmark-iteration boundaries, with a cross-ISA arm on Apple~M4. A working pilot exercises the full pipeline and shows a 27$\times$ package-energy difference between virtual threads and a fixed pool on an I/O-bound workload. That gap is expected by construction from the pool's worker cap, won at \emph{higher} average power over a far shorter runtime; its value is evidence that the pipeline cleanly resolves known effects. The study aims to deliver workload-specific guidance on construct choice and core placement for the large deployed base of server-side Java.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
How a Java program manages its threads shapes how much energy it consumes, yet the standard reference on the energy behavior of Java's thread management constructs is more than a decade old. Pinto, Castor, and Liu measured explicit threads, fixed-size thread pools, and work stealing (Fork--Join) on JDK~1.7, on two homogeneous AMD machines, with external current meters; for six of their nine benchmarks the \emph{sequential} version consumed the least energy, and they concluded that ``being faster clearly has little correlation with being greener''~\cite{pinto2014energy}.

Two shifts since then make that baseline unsafe to extrapolate. First, JDK~21 (September 2023) finalized \emph{virtual threads}~\cite{jep444}: an M:N model that multiplexes a large number of cheap virtual threads over a small carrier \texttt{ForkJoinPool}, unmounting a virtual thread from its carrier whenever it blocks so the carrier can run other work. This changes the energy question qualitatively: where the 2014 constructs traded off thread-creation cost against pool sizing, virtual threads decouple the number of logical threads from the number of OS threads keeping cores busy, so the relationship between concurrency level and active-core power must be re-measured rather than inferred. Second, mainstream CPUs became \emph{hybrid}: Intel has shipped performance/efficiency (P/E) core mixes since 2021, and current parts add a third, low-power-efficiency (LP-E) tier. The carrier pool's work-stealing scheduler treats all carriers as equivalent, but the same unit of work costs different amounts of energy on a P-core than on an LP-E core. Nobody has measured what that blindness costs.

\textbf{No published study measures the energy consumption of JEP~444 virtual threads against platform threads, fixed thread pools, and Fork--Join on JDK~21+, and none on a hybrid processor with heterogeneous core types.} Three observations support this gap. (a)~The 2014 baseline~\cite{pinto2014energy} predates both Project Loom and hybrid cores. (b)~The closest hybrid-core JVM energy work~\cite{shimchenko2024scheduling} varies only the placement of garbage-collection worker threads and touches no application-level concurrency construct. (c)~The only paper attributing energy effects to virtual threads~\cite{shanjaikumar2026virtualthreads} compares just two arms on a microcontroller and reports a software stack---OpenJDK~21 with ZGC on an STM32F407VG with 192\,KB of SRAM---that cannot run as described, since ZGC requires a 64-bit address space; we read it as evidence that the construct-level comparison remains effectively unmeasured.

The study also targets a live scientific dispute: whether faster is greener. Pinto et al.\ answered no~\cite{pinto2014energy}; van Kempen et al.\ recently answered yes at the language level, finding power statistically constant once active cores and memory activity are controlled, while explicitly flagging architectures mixing energy-saving and high-performance cores as a case where their results may differ~\cite{vankempen2025green}; racing-to-idle can cost several times the optimal energy~\cite{kim2015racing}; and a compiler-flag study found faster \emph{and} substantially more energy-hungry code~\cite{aragonjurado2025faster}. We test this dispute on the hardware class where the strongest ``yes'' result itself cautions its conclusions may not hold.

The remainder of this proposal presents the technical and societal context (Section~\ref{sec:context}), positions the study against related work (Section~\ref{sec:related}), states the research design as a GQM tree with pilot feasibility evidence (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and outlines next steps (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\section{Context}\label{sec:context}
\subsection{Technical Context}\label{sec:technical}
\emph{Constructs.} We compare four thread management constructs on JDK~21+: (i)~platform threads created per task; (ii)~fixed-size thread pools (\texttt{newFixedThreadPool}); (iii)~Fork--Join work stealing; and (iv)~virtual threads (thread-per-task over a carrier pool)~\cite{jep444}. The first three are the constructs of the 2014 baseline~\cite{pinto2014energy}; the fourth did not exist then. Figure~\ref{fig:constructs} contrasts two of them: the call sites are identical, the thread economics radically different, which is what makes a fair energy comparison possible. The constructs differ in the properties that plausibly drive energy: how many OS threads exist (per-task creation can oversubscribe the machine, a fixed pool caps it), how work is distributed (central queue versus per-worker deques with stealing), and what happens on blocking (a parked platform thread remains an OS-scheduled thread; a blocked virtual thread unmounts, freeing its carrier).

\begin{figure}
\begin{verbatim}
// 2014-era: bounded platform threads
try (var pool = Executors
      .newFixedThreadPool(8)) {
    pool.invokeAll(tasks);
}
// JDK 21: one virtual thread per task
try (var pool = Executors
      .newVirtualThreadPerTaskExecutor()) {
    pool.invokeAll(tasks);
}
\end{verbatim}

\label{fig:constructs}

\emph{Platform.} The primary platform is an Intel Core Ultra~7 255H, a hybrid CPU with \emph{three} core tiers and no SMT: 6 P-cores (up to 5.1\,GHz, Linux capacity 1024), 8 E-cores (4.4\,GHz, 774), and 2 LP-E cores (2.5\,GHz, 312). The capacity values are the OS scheduler's own estimate of relative per-core throughput; the spread from 312 to 1024 quantifies how asymmetric the machine is that the JVM treats as uniform. A secondary platform, Apple~M4 (P/E cores, measured with \texttt{powermetrics}), provides a cross-ISA arm to check whether findings are Intel-specific.

\emph{Measurement.} Energy is read from the RAPL counters exposed via the Linux powercap interface, at four domains: package (the whole CPU die), core (the cores alone), uncore, and psys (platform scope where available). Reporting per domain lets us separate effects on the cores from effects on shared structures. The measurement itself can perturb the system: high-frequency RAPL polling suppresses C-states and can inflate idle power by tens of watts at kilohertz rates~\cite{raffin2025dissecting}. Our profiler therefore reads counters \emph{only at benchmark-iteration boundaries}, adding no periodic wake-ups during the measured region.

\subsection{Societal Context and Sustainability}\label{sec:societal}
We frame impact with the five-dimension sustainability model of the Karlskrona Manifesto~\cite{becker2015karlskrona}. \emph{Environmental:} data centres consumed an estimated 415\,TWh (about 1.5\% of global electricity) in 2024, a figure projected to roughly double by 2030~\cite{iea2025energyai}; Java is one of the dominant server-side languages in that fleet, so per-operation energy differences multiply across long-running services. \emph{Economic:} energy is a first-order datacenter cost, and battery life a product constraint on laptops and mobile devices; both now ship hybrid CPUs. \emph{Technical:} virtual threads improve maintainability by letting simple blocking code replace complex asynchronous code. \emph{Individual:} the same shift lowers developer cognitive load, since blocking code is easier to write, read, and debug than callback- or future-based code. \emph{Social:} the guidance serves Java's developer community, which today must choose among these constructs with no energy data at all.

These dimensions are in \emph{tension}, and we name one explicitly: virtual threads improve technical and individual sustainability (a simple blocking style), but may \emph{harm} environmental sustainability if near-free threads encourage over-parallelism that raises active-core counts and package power. Whether that trade-off is real is what the study quantifies; the pilot (Section~\ref{sec:feasibility}) already shows the interaction at work, with the lowest-energy construct drawing \emph{higher} average power over a shorter runtime.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
We position the proposal against eight studies; Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes the coverage gap.

\emph{Pinto et al.}~\cite{pinto2014energy} is our baseline: three constructs on JDK~1.7, measured with external NI data-acquisition current meters at 100 samples/s on two homogeneous AMD machines (primarily a 2$\times$16-core Opteron~6378) under the \texttt{ondemand} governor. They attribute the rising energy segment at low-to-mid thread counts, before the peak of the $\Lambda$ curve, to idle-core DVFS behavior under \texttt{ondemand}. \emph{Limitation:} JDK~7 on homogeneous hardware, no on-chip (RAPL) measurement, and results confounded with a governor long since replaced.

\emph{Shimchenko et al.}~\cite{shimchenko2024scheduling} is the closest hybrid-core JVM energy study and our methodological template for P/E-core work: pinning ZGC worker threads to E-cores on Alder Lake saved about 3\% energy, measured with RAPL. \emph{Limitation:} it varies only runtime (GC) threads, not application-level concurrency constructs.

\emph{Shanjai Kumar et al.}~\cite{shanjaikumar2026virtualthreads} is the only paper attributing energy differences to virtual threads. \emph{Limitation:} it compares only two arms, on a microcontroller, and reports a stack (OpenJDK~21 with ZGC on 192\,KB of SRAM) that cannot run as described, leaving the construct-level comparison effectively unvalidated.

\emph{van Kempen et al.}~\cite{vankempen2025green} show that across 13 language implementations, power was statistically constant ($\approx$189.8\,W) once active cores and memory activity were controlled, so energy tracks time. \emph{Limitation:} a homogeneous Xeon with CLBG, SPEC CPU 2017, and DaCapo benchmarks, and the authors explicitly flag ``architectures mixing energy-saving and high-performance cores'' (our platform) as a case where their results may differ.

\emph{Pereira et al.}~\cite{pereira2017energy} is the standard language-energy ranking. \emph{Limitation:} instrumentation defects (counter-overflow mishandling, negative energy readings in the released data) documented by van Kempen et al.~\cite{vankempen2025green}.

\emph{Kim et al.}~\cite{kim2015racing} analyze race-to-idle heuristics: racing can cost up to 3.34$\times$ the optimal energy, and was worst on the platform with the \emph{lowest} idle power. \emph{Limitation:} a DVFS-under-deadline framing, not language-level constructs.

\emph{Arag{\'o}n-Jurado et al.}~\cite{aragonjurado2025faster} found a compiler-flag combination that made an iOS application 9\% faster and 34\% \emph{more} energy-hungry. \emph{Limitation:} compiler flags on iOS, not concurrency constructs.

\emph{Raffin and Trystram}~\cite{raffin2025dissecting} dissect software energy measurement and show that high-frequency RAPL polling suppresses C-states, inflating idle power by tens of watts at 1\,kHz. \emph{Limitation:} pure measurement methodology; it motivates our boundary-only read design but answers no concurrency question.

\emph{Novelty.} No prior study combines construct-level comparison, virtual threads, JDK~21+, and heterogeneous cores (Table~\ref{tab:related}); we occupy exactly that empty row.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
The study is a controlled comparative experiment: four constructs $\times$ two workload classes (CPU-bound and I/O-bound) $\times$ a sweep of concurrency levels, run unpinned and under fixed core placements (P, E, LP-E) on the three-tier hybrid CPU described in Section~\ref{sec:context}, with energy read from RAPL at iteration boundaries and a cross-ISA arm on Apple~M4. We structure the design with the Goal--Question--Metric approach.

\subsection{Goal--Question--Metric}\label{sec:gqm}
We state the general goal in the Basili template~\cite{basili1994gqm}:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Analyze} Java thread management constructs (platform threads, fixed thread pools, Fork--Join, and virtual threads) \textbf{for the purpose of} characterizing \textbf{with respect to} their energy consumption and its relationship to execution time \textbf{from the point of view of} Java developers \textbf{in the context of} hybrid processors combining performance, efficiency, and low-power-efficiency cores.
\end{quote}

\noindent Three specific goals refine it.

\emph{G1 --- Re-establish the baseline.} Does the 2014 ``Lambda curve'' (energy as a function of concurrency level) persist under modern \texttt{intel\_pstate}/HWP frequency governance on hybrid cores? Pinto et al.\ attributed part of the curve's rising segment to how the then-current \texttt{ondemand} governor scaled idle cores~\cite{pinto2014energy}; hardware-managed P-states may have changed the shape entirely.
\begin{itemize}
\item[Q1.1] What is the shape of energy consumption versus concurrency level for each construct?
\item[Q1.2] Is sequential execution still the lowest-energy option for most workloads?
\end{itemize}

\emph{G2 --- Position virtual threads.} Where do virtual threads sit in the energy landscape relative to classical constructs?
\begin{itemize}
\item[Q2.1] How does virtual-thread energy consumption compare per workload class (CPU-bound vs.\ I/O-bound)?
\item[Q2.2] At what concurrency level, if any, do virtual threads become the lowest-energy construct?
\end{itemize}

\emph{G3 --- Scheduler asymmetry blindness.} Does the OS/JVM scheduler's unawareness of core asymmetry cost energy? Pinning GC threads alone to E-cores has been shown to save energy~\cite{shimchenko2024scheduling}; G3 asks the corresponding question for application threads on a three-tier part.
\begin{itemize}
\item[Q3.1] Does pinning execution to P, E, or LP-E cores significantly change energy per unit of work?
\item[Q3.2] How much energy does the asymmetry-unaware scheduler leave on the table compared with the best fixed core placement?
\end{itemize}

\emph{Preliminary metrics} (to be refined in Assignment~2), mapped to the goals. G1: package, core, uncore, and psys energy (J); execution time (s); energy per operation (J/op); and energy-delay product. G2: J/op per strategy~$\times$ parallelism~$\times$ workload combination; throughput (ops/s); and Cliff's delta effect size against the fixed-pool baseline. G3: J/op per core class (P/E/LP-E); time-to-completion penalty (\%); and energy saving of the best fixed placement versus unpinned scheduling (\%).

\subsection{Feasibility}\label{sec:feasibility}
A pilot implements the full pipeline: JMH benchmarks with a custom RAPL profiler that reads counters only at iteration boundaries (avoiding the C-state suppression of high-frequency polling~\cite{raffin2025dissecting}); parallelism~8, unpinned, 2 forks $\times$ 10 measured iterations, 256 tasks per operation; the I/O-bound workload is 10\,ms sleeps and the CPU-bound workload a Mandelbrot computation.

On the I/O-bound workload, virtual threads completed an operation in 10.3\,ms using 0.144\,J of package energy, against 323.5\,ms and 3.86\,J/op for the fixed pool: a 27$\times$ energy reduction, achieved at \emph{higher} average power (14.0\,W vs.\ 11.9\,W) but far shorter runtime. That gap is expected by construction: pushing 256 fully blocking 10\,ms sleeps through 8 workers must take at least $256/8 \times 10\,\mathrm{ms} = 320$\,ms, while virtual threads overlap all 256 sleeps. The agreement between this arithmetic floor and the observed 323.5\,ms is itself a validity check on the pipeline; the pilot's contribution is showing that the measurement resolves this known effect cleanly, not discovering a new phenomenon. On the CPU-bound workload, virtual threads used 3.38\,J/op against the fixed pool's 5.13\,J/op; however, the pool arms were capped at 8 workers while the thread-per-task arms could span all 16 cores, so this gap partly reflects core count, an asymmetry the full design will control. The pilot thus demonstrates the pipeline end to end and already exhibits the race-to-idle dynamic (faster at higher power, winning on energy) that the study will characterize systematically.

\subsection{Expected Results and Impact}
We expect three kinds of outcome. First, \emph{actionable guidance} for practitioners: which construct to choose per workload class, and possibly a carrier-pinning recommendation: if pinning GC threads alone is worth about 3\%~\cite{shimchenko2024scheduling}, placing all application carriers may be worth more, though we make no prediction of the sign. Second, a \emph{replication-style update} of the 2014 baseline~\cite{pinto2014energy} on hardware and JDKs a decade newer. Third, a position in the faster-is-greener dispute on the hardware class where the strongest ``faster is greener'' evidence itself cautions its conclusions may not hold~\cite{vankempen2025green}. Every outcome is informative: confirmation of the 2014 findings, a null result bounded by equivalence tests, and a reversal all answer the research questions.

\section{Concluding Remarks}\label{sec:conclusion}
This proposal identifies a concrete, falsifiable gap (no construct-level energy measurement of virtual threads on JDK~21+, and none on heterogeneous cores) and a GQM-structured experiment to close it, with a working pilot as feasibility evidence. The pilot's headline 27$\times$ difference is expected by construction (Section~\ref{sec:feasibility}); the full design will separate such known effects from genuine construct differences.

Next steps. \emph{Assignment~2} delivers the full experimental design: the complete variable set, including core-pinning arms (P, E, and LP-E cores via \texttt{taskset}) and control of the carrier count through the \texttt{jdk.virtualThreadScheduler.}\allowbreak\texttt{parallelism} property; a real-socket I/O workload replacing the pilot's sleeps; idle-baseline subtraction; and a statistical plan built on non-parametric tests with equivalence testing for null results. \emph{Assignment~3} executes the experiment, including the Apple~M4 arm, analyzes the results, and publishes a replication package.
